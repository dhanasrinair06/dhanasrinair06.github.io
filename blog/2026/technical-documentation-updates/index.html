Technical documentation updates — and where that annual requirement actually comes from

Created on August 30, 2026

2026   ·   regulatory-affairs   technical-documentation   ·   regulatory-notes

Technical file (TF) and technical documentation (TD) updates sit at the centre of every change impact assessment, and they come round again each year regardless of whether anything has changed. How significant a change is determines whether it lands as a substantial or a minor revision to the TD/TF.

What counts as a significant change. Some changes clearly push a TF update into substantial territory:

  • Sterilization method — for example, moving from ethylene oxide to electron beam radiation
  • Device design
  • Intended purpose
  • Patient population
  • Critical materials that come into contact with the device
  • Anything affecting the overall safety of the device

Food for thought: where does the annual update requirement actually come from? No regulation explicitly states that you must update your TF/TD every year. The requirement is cumulative; it emerges from reading several clauses together.

Under MDR:

  • Article 86(1) requires manufacturers of Class IIb and Class III devices to update the PSUR at least annually, and the regulation states that the PSUR forms part of the technical documentation under Annexes II and III.
  • Article 61(11) requires the clinical evaluation and its documentation to be updated throughout the device lifecycle using PMCF and PMS data. For Class III and implantable devices, the PMCF Evaluation Report and the SSCP, where indicated, must be updated at least annually.

Under MDD:

  • MDD 93/42/EEC states that the clinical evaluation and its documentation must be actively updated with data obtained from post-market surveillance.
  • MDD-certified products today are generally legacy devices under MDR Article 120. MDCG 2021-25 Rev.1 confirms that, in accordance with Article 120(3d), the MDR requirements relating to post-market surveillance, market surveillance, vigilance, and registration of economic operators and devices apply to legacy devices — a provision unchanged by Regulation 2023/607.
  • Follow that back to Article 86, and the annual update applies here too.

Taken together, a 12-month update cycle for the TD/TF is the approach that holds up.

Start from what triggered the change. Read the new change being introduced, how it prompted a regulatory action (change notice/technical file update), and note every deliverable that falls out of it. That list becomes the spine of the update.

Update the documents that hang off the change. Validation/aging/quality inspection reports, product specifications, the GSPRC, labeling. Work through each one and either update it or create it new so it reflects the change.

Bring subject matter experts in early. Get the right reviewer onto the right section: a sterilization SME on the sterilization sections; a regulatory compliance review of the standards and GSPRC clauses the device is declared against with its evidence of completion. Early alignment saves rework later.

Redline everything, then get it peer-reviewed. Capture every change in redlines, with background information where it helps. Peer review is the critical step; it’s what confirms the changes were captured accurately and that the change history carries sufficient rationale.

Don’t release until the deliverables are closed. Before releasing the technical documentation into the regulatory repository, confirm that all reports, document and labeling updates are complete and released in the quality system. If something is still pending, either work with the change owner to close it first, or assess the risk and agree the strategy from there.

Expect the Declaration of Conformity to follow. The next deliverable after a TD update is usually the impact to the DoC, and the change type normally answers it for you. Adding a manufacturing site, for example, changes the content of the DoC, which means it has to be re-signed by the authorized signatory to stay compliant with the latest changes.
